Plankton?! What is plankton?!

Plankton are those organisms that are mainly transported by currents in the sea. There are two main groups: phytoplankton and zooplankton. Phytoplankton (from the Greek phytos = plant) obtain energy from sunlight through photosynthesis (see equation below). Zooplankton (from the Greek zōion = animal), on the other hand, are heterotrophic, meaning they consume other organisms to obtain energy (just like humans). Because phytoplankton perform photosynthesis, they produce oxygen as a by-product. Remarkably, about 50% of the oxygen in Earth’s atmosphere is generated by phytoplankton. Also, from the equation, it becomes clear that phytoplankton use CO2, hence removing carbon from the atmosphere and using it as building blocks to produce body mass (organic carbon).

Turbidity and zooplankton feeding

As you have noticed, it took longer to catch the same amount of prey as before. This is because sand particles make it more difficult for the copepod to catch the prey as it interferes with the detection of the prey, and leads to accidental ingestion of sand, taking up space in the gut. Also, because of this sand ingestion, the carbon content of fecal pellet is reduced, diminishing their potential to store carbon in the seafloor.

Other effects of sand extraction?

Sand extraction does not only introduce sand particles (turbidity) in the water column, but also has the potential to alter flow conditions, introducing turbulence. Turbulence is the irregular movement of water. While low levels of turbulence have no or little impact, high levels make it difficult for the copepod to detect and catch prey.Try it yourself and experience the effect of high turbulence in combination with turbidity!

Turbidity + turbulence effects

As you can see, the combination of high turbulence and turbidity makes it difficult to detect and catch prey. Even though copepods are the fastest creatures on the planet (size corrected), they are sensitive to changes in their environment. As they are part of the foundation of marine food webs, effects observed here will echo throughout the marine food web. Want to find out more? Read Gerard et al., 2026.

Zooplankton feeding: feces

When copepods eat, they produce fecal pellets. These pellets are rich in nutrients (such as N and P) and are quickly colonized by bacteria. These pellets are then sometimes eaten again by zooplankton (that they just pooped out!). The process of eating feces (poops) is called coprophagia. A famous, known example of this are bunnies, who also re-ingest their own feces! If the pellets are not eaten, they slowly sink and are deposited on the seafloor, where they are either remineralized to inorganic nutrients (making the nutrients available again for phytplankton), taken up by benthic organisms (orgamisms living on or just below the seafloor, just like the happy crab in our game!) or burried in the seafloor. Because fecal pellets also contain carbon (the pellets consist of phytoplankton prey that as we now use CO2 to build body mass). Copepods, by just eating and pooping, are very important in storing carbon in our seas!

zooplankton predators and the food chain

Copepods are very good at capturing prey, but they should be cautious as they are also on the menu of other orgamisms such as fish. Fish that consumes plankton is called planktivorous fish. These planktivorous fish are in return on the menu of fish that eats other fish (pescivorous fish).They in turn are on the menu of bigger fish, marine mammels and even humans! This cascade of who eats who is what we call a food chain. The higher an organism is in this food chain, the higher the 'trophic level'of this organism. A change at the basis of this food chain (plankton) can thus have far reaching consequences!

Sand extraction, what is it and what does it do?

It is estimated the Netherlands needs to drastically increase marine sand extraction to use this sand to make concrete to reinforce dikes, built roads, houses, but also use the sand to nourish (maintain) dunes and beaches.This sand extraction is done by ships called trailer-suction-hopper-dredgers. Basically, these ships are giant vacuums that 'vacuum' the sand of the seafloor and store the sand on board. By doing this, a lot of sand gets released in the water column. Sand and by extension all particles in the water column are together called turbidity. Turbidity is not only introduced in the water by sand extraction, but also by river run-off, bottom trawling (a fisheries technique) and stroms that cause upwelling. It is now up to you to experience how elevated turbidity impacts you as a copepod!
